Secure CAN Monitor · Raspberry Pi 3 · OP-TEE

Results: all 32 test cases on the real two-Pi CAN-FD bus, in five groups. A boot and setup, 4 tests; B normal traffic, 3; C attacks, 10; D load and loss, 12; E restart and reboot, 3. Blue tiles passed the first time, 31 of them. The orange tile, E2b, did not pass the first time: the forwarder did not start by itself at boot. It was fixed with a new image that starts the forwarder as a boot service, and it was re-tested and passes now, so all 32 now have a passing result. Be precise if asked: the 31 blue tests ran on the earlier image; E2b was re-run on the new image, together with A1, A4, B1 and A2 on that image. After the fix one new limit appeared, E2c: controlling the service over ssh does not return; the workaround is documented and the fix is queued for the next image. Twelve limits are documented in the test report, none hidden. Right side, the numbers: nothing is lost up to 640 frames per second and in bursts of about 2,490 frames; above that every drop is counted. All 18 load runs of the loss series matched exactly; 12 of them dropped frames, and in those 12 the counted drops equal the gap the Secure World sees; 278,204 frames were accounted. Loss under load, totals over 3 runs each: the 1,004-frame burst lost nothing; steady 943 frames per second dropped 9,203 of 42,000; a flood of 3,587 frames per second dropped 80,040 of 105,000. The accounting rule: sent equals judged plus counted losses. Trust proofs checked on the board on 25 November: Linux sees only cores 0, 2 and 3 while core 1 keeps running; the Secure World memory is reserved in the Linux memory map; the forwarder and the monitor client contain no key. The keys are prototype keys built into the image, and the monitor detects but does not block, because the Pi 3 has no TZASC. By group on the first run it was A 4 of 4, B 3 of 3, C 10 of 10, D 12 of 12, E 2 of 3.
